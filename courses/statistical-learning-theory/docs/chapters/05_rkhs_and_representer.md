# RKHS and the Representer Theorem

**Source:** 598SLT.pdf, pp. 18-23.

## 1. Regularized soft-margin objective

From the hinge-loss formulation, the soft-margin SVM in a Hilbert space solves

$$\min_{w\in\mathcal{H},b}\;\frac{1}{2}\langle w,w\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}+C\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Phi\left(y_{i}\left(\langle w,\phi(x_{i})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}+b\right)\right).$$

Dividing the objective by $C$ does not change its minimizer. The notes therefore write the equivalent regularized form

$$\min_{w\in\mathcal{H},b}\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Phi\left(y_{i}\left(\langle w,\phi(x_{i})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}+b\right)\right)+\lambda\lVert w\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}^{2},$$

with

$$\lambda=\frac{1}{2C}.$$

The Hilbert-space norm is induced by the inner product:

$$\lVert u\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}=\langle u,u\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}.$$

The course now asks which Hilbert space should be used for kernel learning.

## 2. Finite linear combinations generated by a function

Let

$$h:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}.$$

For each $u\in\mathcal{X}$, the expression $h(\cdot,u)$ is a function of its first argument. Define

$$\mathcal{H}_{0}=\left\lbrace \sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}h(\cdot,u_{i})\;\middle|\;m\geq 1,\;\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{R},\;u_{i}\in\mathcal{X}\right\rbrace.$$

Thus, $\mathcal{H}_{0}$ contains every finite linear combination of the functions $h(\cdot,u)$.

!!! clarification "What are the elements of $\mathcal{H}_{0}$?"
    They are functions on the input space, not ordinary finite-coordinate vectors. A single element $f\in\mathcal{H}_{0}$ may be written as $f(\cdot)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}h(\cdot,u_{i})$.

## 3. Positive-definite functions

### Definition 3: Positive-definite function

A symmetric function $h:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}$ is positive definite when, for every $m\geq 1$, every $u_{1},\ldots,u_{m}\in\mathcal{X}$, and every $\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m}\in\mathbb{R}$,

$$\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}h(u_{i},u_{j})\geq 0.$$

This is the function analogue of a positive-semidefinite matrix: every finite Gram matrix formed from $h$ has a nonnegative quadratic form.

## 4. Inner product on $\mathcal{H}_{0}$

Let

$$f=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}h(\cdot,u_{i})$$

and

$$g=\sum_{j=1}^{m'}\beta_{j}h(\cdot,v_{j}).$$

The course defines

$$\langle f,g\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m'}\alpha_{i}\beta_{j}h(u_{i},v_{j}).$$

### Symmetry

Because $h(u,v)=h(v,u)$,

$$\langle f,g\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}=\langle g,f\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}.$$

### Linearity

The double-sum definition is linear in the coefficients, so

$$\langle af_{1}+bf_{2},g\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}=a\langle f_{1},g\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}+b\langle f_{2},g\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}.$$

### Nonnegativity

For

$$f=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}h(\cdot,u_{i}),$$

positive definiteness gives

$$\langle f,f\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}h(u_{i},u_{j})\geq 0.$$

!!! note "Why is positive definiteness needed?"
    It guarantees that the proposed squared norm cannot be negative. The remaining requirement, $\langle f,f\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}=0\Rightarrow f=0$, and the fact that the result is independent of the chosen finite representation of $f$, require a more careful construction. Page 20 cites this result from the reference material and states that its proof is not required for the course.

## 5. The reproducing property

For $x\in\mathcal{X}$, the function $h(\cdot,x)$ belongs to $\mathcal{H}_{0}$. If

$$f=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}h(\cdot,u_{i}),$$

then

$$\langle f,h(\cdot,x)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}h(u_{i},x)=f(x).$$

This identity is the reproducing property:

$$f(x)=\langle f,h(\cdot,x)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}.$$

!!! clarification "Why is it called reproducing?"
    The inner product with the special function $h(\cdot,x)$ reproduces the value of $f$ at $x$. Function evaluation is therefore encoded by the geometry of the space.

## 6. Completion and the RKHS

The space $\mathcal{H}_{0}$ contains finite linear combinations, but it need not be complete. The course defines $\mathcal{H}$ as the completion of $\mathcal{H}_{0}$.

Informally, $\mathcal{H}$ is the smallest closed space containing $\mathcal{H}_{0}$ and preserving the same inner product. The notes emphasize that the technical construction uses topology and functional analysis and is beyond the required scope.

The resulting space $\mathcal{H}$ is called the reproducing-kernel Hilbert space associated with $h$.

!!! note "Course-level simplification"
    For the subsequent derivations, the notes say that one may think of $\mathcal{H}$ as almost the same as $\mathcal{H}_{0}$ while remembering that $\mathcal{H}$ also contains limits required for completeness.

## 7. Kernels are positive definite

Suppose a kernel has a feature representation

$$K(x,x')=\langle\phi(x),\phi(x')\rangle_{\mathcal{H}'},$$

for some Hilbert space $\mathcal{H}'$. Then, for any coefficients $\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m}$ and points $u_{1},\ldots,u_{m}$,

$$\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}K(u_{i},u_{j})=\left\lVert\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}\phi(u_{i})\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}'}^{2}\geq 0.$$

Therefore, every kernel is a positive-definite function. Replacing $h$ by $K$ in the preceding construction gives an RKHS associated with $K$.

!!! clarification "Are $\mathcal{H}'$ and the constructed RKHS the same space?"
    The source explicitly warns that they should not automatically be identified. $\mathcal{H}'$ is one feature space that realizes the kernel as an inner product. The RKHS $\mathcal{H}$ is the function space constructed from the kernel sections $K(\cdot,x)$.

## 8. Soft-margin SVM in an RKHS

Given a kernel $K$, let $\mathcal{H}$ be its associated RKHS. The transformed feature of $x$ can be represented by the kernel section

$$\phi(x)=K(\cdot,x).$$

The soft-margin objective becomes

$$\min_{w\in\mathcal{H},b}\;\frac{1}{2}\lVert w\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}+C\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Phi\left(y_{i}\left(\langle w,K(\cdot,x_{i})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}+b\right)\right).$$

By the reproducing property,

$$\langle w,K(\cdot,x_{i})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=w(x_{i}).$$

An important conceptual point from the notes is that each element $w\in\mathcal{H}$ is itself a function defined on $\mathcal{X}$.

## 9. The representer theorem

### Theorem 2: Representer theorem

For the regularized empirical objective above, there exist coefficients $\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}\in\mathbb{R}$ such that an optimizer satisfies

$$w^{\star}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\phi(x_{i}).$$

In the RKHS representation, this can be written as

$$w^{\star}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}K(\cdot,x_{i}).$$

Thus, although $\mathcal{H}$ may be infinite-dimensional, the optimizer lies in the span of the $n$ transformed training examples.

## 10. Proof idea in the finite-dimensional case

The course proves a simpler version without feature mapping. Consider

$$\min_{w\in\mathbb{R}^{d},b}\;\frac{1}{2}\lVert w\rVert_{2}^{2}+C\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Phi\left(y_{i}\left(w^{\top}x_{i}+b\right)\right).$$

Define the training-data span

$$\mathcal{A}=\left\lbrace \sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}x_{i}\;\middle|\;\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{R}\right\rbrace.$$

Every $w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ can be decomposed as

$$w=w_{1}+w_{2},$$

where

$$w_{1}\in\mathcal{A}$$

and

$$w_{2}\perp\mathcal{A}.$$

Because each $x_{i}\in\mathcal{A}$,

$$w_{2}^{\top}x_{i}=0,$$

so

$$w^{\top}x_{i}=w_{1}^{\top}x_{i}.$$

The entire hinge-loss term depends only on $w_{1}$. Meanwhile, orthogonality gives

$$\lVert w\rVert_{2}^{2}=\lVert w_{1}\rVert_{2}^{2}+\lVert w_{2}\rVert_{2}^{2}.$$

If $w_{2}\neq 0$, replacing $w$ by $w_{1}$ leaves every training prediction unchanged and strictly decreases the regularization term. Therefore, an optimizer must satisfy

$$w_{2}^{\star}=0.$$

Hence,

$$w^{\star}=w_{1}^{\star}\in\mathcal{A},$$

which means

$$w^{\star}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}x_{i}.$$

The notes state that the general representer theorem follows the same orthogonal-decomposition strategy in the Hilbert space, but the full proof is not required.

!!! clarification "Why does the theorem matter computationally?"
    The optimization can be expressed through $n$ coefficients associated with the training data rather than through arbitrary coordinates of an infinite-dimensional function. This is the structural reason kernel methods can remain finite computational problems.

## Chapter summary

- Positive-definite functions generate nonnegative Gram-matrix quadratic forms.
- Finite linear combinations of kernel sections form a pre-Hilbert space.
- The reproducing property turns function evaluation into an inner product.
- Completing the space gives an RKHS.
- Every kernel is positive definite, and every positive-definite kernel has an associated RKHS.
- The representer theorem restricts the regularized optimizer to the span of the training features.
